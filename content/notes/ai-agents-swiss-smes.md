Short answer: yes, for well-defined work. Not yet for running a business on its own.

An AI agent is more than a chatbot. It reads, decides on a next step and acts: it sorts an inbox, pulls the figures out of an invoice, drafts a reply, prepares a booking. In 2026 this moved from experiments into daily use. What has not changed is that agents are only as good as the process you give them.

## Where agents work well today

- **Documents in, structured data out.** Invoices, receipts, tax documents, contracts: reading them and filling a system is repetitive, rule-based and easy to check.
- **First drafts.** Replies to common customer questions, offers from a template, meeting notes. A person reviews and sends.
- **Preparation, not decisions.** Collecting what is missing before a case starts: which documents are there, which are not, what to ask the client.

## Where they still need a human

Anything that costs money, goes to a client or cannot be undone. Agents make mistakes that look convincing. The answer is not to avoid them but to put a human approval at exactly these points.

## How to start

1. **Pick one process.** Repetitive, frequent, with a clear result. Not "use AI", but "every incoming invoice is captured within an hour".
2. **Write down the limits.** What may the agent read? What may it change? What must it never do?
3. **Keep the approval step.** The agent prepares, a person confirms. Remove the step only when the error rate has been low for months.
4. **Measure before and after.** Minutes per case and errors per month. Without numbers, every project feels like a success.

## Data protection in Switzerland

Since 1 September 2023 the revised Federal Act on Data Protection (FADP) applies. For an agent that touches client data, three questions matter: where is the data processed, is it used to train someone else's model, and can you explain to a client what happens with their information? Choose providers and settings so that you can answer all three in one sentence each.
